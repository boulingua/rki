## Why this level exists

A learner of French or English can read the first page of the first unit on
the first day. A learner of Russian cannot. Thirty-three letters stand between
them and the language, and a third of those letters are traps: **В Н Р С У Х**
are shaped like Latin letters and sound like something else, **ь** and **ъ**
are not sounds at all, and the cursive that Russian speakers actually write by
hand is a second alphabet layered on the first.

Courses that treat this as a warm-up spend the whole of A1 paying for it:
learners decode instead of reading, and every vocabulary item costs twice. So
the script gets its own level, its own materials and its own check, and A1
begins only when the check is passed.

## The five units

Level 0 groups the alphabet by difficulty rather than by the dictionary order,
so that a learner can read real words after the first unit rather than after
the fifth.

| № | Unit | Focus |
|---|------|-------|
| 1 | Гла́сные и пе́рвые согла́сные | The vowel system, the letters that look and sound like their Latin counterparts, first readable words |
| 2 | Ло́жные друзья́: В Н Р С У Х | The six letters whose Latin shape misleads; drilled to automaticity before anything is built on them |
| 3 | Остальны́е согла́сные, ь и ъ | The remaining consonants, the hushing letters, and the two signs that mark palatalisation and separation rather than sound |
| 4 | Печа́тный шрифт, курси́в и про́пись | Print against italic against cursive — including the italic forms (*т*, *п*, *г*, *д*) that catch every beginner — and handwriting |
| 5 | Бе́глое чте́ние и клавиату́ра | Reading at speed, syllable division, and typing: ЙЦУКЕН against a phonetic layout |

Each unit ships the standard bundle: an HTML article, a slide deck, a
printable worksheet with a handwriting grid, an answer key and audio for every
letter, syllable and word introduced.

## The readiness check

Level 0 closes with a **script-readiness check** built in the same shape as
the level exams: a fixed duration, a point total, a marking scale, and a
printable PDF. It tests four things and nothing else — letter recognition in
all three type styles, reading aloud of unseen words, dictation of heard
words, and typing a short sentence in Cyrillic. Grammar is not tested; there
is no grammar yet.

{{< callout type="note" title="What a learner can do when Level 0 is passed" >}}
Decode any Russian word aloud, including one they have never seen; recognise
all thirty-three letters in print, italic and cursive; write them by hand;
type Russian on a Cyrillic layout; and know that **ё** is a letter in its own
right.
{{< /callout >}}

## Conventions introduced here

- **Transliteration is a crutch, and it is temporary.** A light,
  ISO-9-flavoured transliteration runs alongside the Cyrillic in Units 1 to 3
  and thins out through Units 4 and 5. It survives into the first A1 units and
  is gone by the end of A1. Learners who keep reading the transliteration stop
  learning to read.
- **Stress is marked from the first word.** `хорошо́`, `спаси́бо`, `молоко́`.
  Russian stress is lexical, mobile and unwritten, and it decides vowel
  quality: unstressed **о** is not [o]. Marking it in teaching text is the only
  honest way to present a word in print.
- **ё is written ё.** Russian publishing routinely prints е for ё; a beginner
  who learns `все` and `всё` as one word learns two errors at once — a wrong
  vowel and a wrong stress, since ё is always stressed and therefore never
  carries an acute.

## Handwriting and the keyboard

Cursive (**про́пись**) is taught, not mentioned. Handwritten Russian is what a
learner will meet on a form, a note or a whiteboard, and several cursive
letters bear no useful resemblance to their printed shapes. Unit 4 supplies
ruled practice sheets, letter by letter and then in joined words.

For typing, both layouts are shown: the standard **ЙЦУКЕН** layout used
throughout the Russian-speaking world, and the phonetic layouts available on
most operating systems, which map Russian letters onto the QWERTZ keys a
German-keyboard learner already knows. The recommendation for a beginner is
the phonetic layout, with ЙЦУКЕН as the target for anyone who will type
Russian regularly.

## Curriculum position

Level 0 sits below A1. Where the CEFR Companion Volume provides **pre-A1**
descriptors that a script-onboarding unit genuinely realises — a handful in
reception and interaction — those are mapped in the usual way. Everything else
here is **script literacy**, which the CEFR does not describe, and it is
recorded as a course-local objective rather than dressed up as a CEFR claim.
Absence of an official descriptor is declared, never faked.
